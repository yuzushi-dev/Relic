# Theoretical Grounding

Relic's facet model draws from six established frameworks in cognitive and personality psychology. This page documents each framework, its contribution to the model, and the relevant literature.

Using these frameworks does not make Relic a psychometric instrument. See [Limitations](limitations.md) for the specific constraints on how these contributions should be interpreted.

## Cognitive Appraisal Theory

**Contribution to Relic**: Appraisal pattern facets, how situations are evaluated along dimensions such as controllability, threat vs. challenge, and agency. Stress-response and coping orientation facets are derived from appraisal dimensions.

**Key references**:

- Lazarus, R. S., & Folkman, S. (1984). *Stress, Appraisal, and Coping*. Springer.
- Lazarus, R. S. (1991). *Emotion and Adaptation*. Oxford University Press.
- Smith, C. A., & Ellsworth, P. C. (1985). Patterns of cognitive appraisal in emotion. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 48(4), 813–838.

## Self-Determination Theory (SDT)

**Contribution to Relic**: Three core need facets, autonomy (self-direction vs. dependence on external structure), competence (efficacy vs. self-doubt), and relatedness (connection vs. isolation). Observable in communication patterns related to initiative, expressed confidence, and relational engagement.

**Key references**:

- Deci, E. L., & Ryan, R. M. (1985). *Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior*. Plenum.
- Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. *American Psychologist*, 55(1), 68–78.
- Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2017). *Self-Determination Theory: Basic Psychological Needs in Motivation, Development, and Wellness*. Guilford Press.

## Attachment Theory

**Contribution to Relic**: Relational style facets derived from attachment dimensions, anxiety (concern about availability and acceptance) and avoidance (discomfort with closeness and reliance). Observable in how subjects discuss relationships, express needs, and respond to relational warmth or distance in interaction.

**Key references**:

- Bowlby, J. (1969). *Attachment and Loss, Vol. 1: Attachment*. Basic Books.
- Ainsworth, M. D. S., Blehar, M. C., Waters, E., & Wall, S. (1978). *Patterns of Attachment*. Erlbaum.
- Brennan, K. A., Clark, C. L., & Shaver, P. R. (1998). Self-report measurement of adult attachment. In J. A. Simpson & W. S. Rholes (Eds.), *Attachment Theory and Close Relationships* (pp. 46–76). Guilford Press.
- Fraley, R. C., Waller, N. G., & Brennan, K. A. (2000). An item response theory analysis of self-report measures of adult attachment. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 78(2), 350–365.

## Dual-Process Theory

**Contribution to Relic**: Facets reflecting the signature balance between fast, associative processing (System 1) and deliberate, effortful processing (System 2). Observable in response patterns, hedging language, revision behavior, and how subjects approach novel or ambiguous problems.

**Key references**:

- Kahneman, D. (2011). *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.
- Evans, J. S. B. T. (2008). Dual-processing accounts of reasoning, judgment, and social cognition. *Annual Review of Psychology*, 59, 255–278.
- Stanovich, K. E., & West, R. F. (2000). Individual differences in reasoning: Implications for the rationality debate. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 23(5), 645–665.

## CAPS (Cognitive-Affective Processing System)

**Contribution to Relic**: Situation-behavior signature modeling. The system tracks which types of situations reliably elicit which behavioral responses for a specific subject, building a within-person signature over time rather than aggregating to a trait mean.

**Key references**:

- Mischel, W., & Shoda, Y. (1995). A cognitive-affective system theory of personality: Reconceptualizing situations, dispositions, dynamics, and invariance in personality structure. *Psychological Review*, 102(2), 246–268.
- Mischel, W., & Shoda, Y. (1998). Reconciling processing dynamics and personality dispositions. *Annual Review of Psychology*, 49, 229–258.

## LIWC (Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count)

**Contribution to Relic**: Linguistic behavioral markers derived from word category analysis. These include psychological process markers (affect words, cognitive process indicators, social words), temporal orientation, and function word patterns associated with specific processing styles.

**Key references**:

- Pennebaker, J. W., Booth, R. J., & Francis, M. E. (2007). *Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count: LIWC2007*. LIWC.net.
- Pennebaker, J. W., Boyd, R. L., Jordan, K., & Blackburn, K. (2015). *The Development and Psychometric Properties of LIWC2015*. University of Texas at Austin.
- Tausczik, Y. R., & Pennebaker, J. W. (2010). The psychological meaning of words: LIWC and computerized text analysis methods. *Journal of Language and Social Psychology*, 29(1), 24–54.

## How these frameworks relate in the model

The six frameworks are not independent modeling layers stacked on top of each other. They overlap and inform the same behavioral observations from different angles. An interaction that looks like high self-efficacy from an SDT perspective may also show System 2 processing signatures in the linguistic analysis, and may be situated in a relational context that activation theory would read as low-threat appraisal.

The model treats these intersections as signal reinforcement rather than redundancy. When multiple frameworks converge on the same facet estimate from different evidence types, confidence increases. When they conflict, the conflict is flagged for researcher review.
