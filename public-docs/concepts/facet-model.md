# Facet Model

Each dimension in a Relic profile is called a facet. Facets represent behavioral tendencies as positions on bipolar spectra — not categories, not scores, not diagnoses.

## Structure of a facet

Every facet has:

- **Position**: a value indicating where the subject tends to fall on the spectrum, based on available evidence.
- **Confidence**: a score from 0.0 to 1.0 indicating how much evidence supports the current estimate.
- **Observation count**: how many data points contributed to the estimate.
- **Source references**: which specific observations support the position.
- **Correction state**: whether the facet has been manually corrected, and what to.

A facet with a confidence score below ~0.3 and a small observation count should be treated as a tentative initial estimate with minimal evidence. Nascent facets are common in early profiles. They exist to structure what the system is watching for, not to make strong claims.

## Theoretical grounding

The facets are derived from frameworks that have been used in research on personality, cognition, and behavior. The frameworks and their contribution to the facet model:

**Cognitive Appraisal Theory** — appraisal patterns: how situations are evaluated (challenging vs. threatening, controllable vs. overwhelming). Stress-response tendencies and coping orientation facets.

**Self-Determination Theory** — three core needs: autonomy (self-direction vs. dependence on external structure), competence (efficacy vs. self-doubt), and relatedness (connection vs. isolation). Each produces a corresponding facet cluster.

**Attachment Theory** — relational style facets derived from attachment dimensions: anxiety (worry about availability and acceptance) and avoidance (discomfort with closeness and reliance). These are observable in communication patterns over time.

**Dual-Process Theory** — behavioral signatures suggesting System 1 (fast, automatic, associative) vs. System 2 (deliberate, effortful, analytical) processing. Observable in response latency patterns, hedging language, and revision tendencies.

**CAPS (Cognitive-Affective Processing System)** — situation-behavior signature modeling. Which types of situations reliably elicit which behavioral responses for this specific person.

**LIWC (Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count)** — linguistic markers of psychological states, cognitive processing, and social orientation, used to ground facet estimates in observable language behavior.

## What facets are not

Using these frameworks does not make Relic an instrument for psychological assessment. The facets are not:

- Validated psychometric scales. The observation context is constrained, the sample is uncontrolled, and no normative data has been collected.
- Clinical indicators. No facet value should be interpreted as evidence of a health condition, a risk factor, or a clinical profile.
- Stable traits. Behavioral tendencies observed in interaction with Gumi may not generalize to other contexts. The model represents behavior within this system.
- Ground truth. The subject's own account of themselves takes precedence over the model. Corrections are authoritative.

## Confidence caps

The system applies explicit upper bounds on confidence for certain facets based on evidence constraints. A facet derived entirely from passive interaction may have a cap lower than one derived from active elicitation combined with self-report. The caps are defined in `relic/patterns/confidence_caps.py`.

## Inferred fields

Some profile extensions are generated by the system without direct evidence — for example, hypotheses about how a subject might respond to a specific type of outreach. These are called inferred fields. They are held at low confidence, labeled as system-inferred, and are not directly injected into runtime context. See `relic/profile/inferred_fields.py` and `tests/profile/test_inferred_fields_*.py`.
